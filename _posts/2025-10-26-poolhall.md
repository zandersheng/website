In [Part 1 of this series](/website/blog/2026/economics-of-cues/), we looked at the microeconomics of the equipment in your hands: why a pool cue can cost anywhere from sub \\$100 to over \\$50,000, and how the market splits into utility tools and Veblen assets. 

Today, we are zooming out from the stick to the room itself. 

If you have lived in any major city over the last twenty years, you have likely watched your favorite traditional pool hall vanish—replaced by luxury apartments, a Sweetgreen, or a boutique fitness studio. Yet at the exact same time, a new breed of pool room is quietly booming in high rent cities from New York and Seattle to Taipei: **the 24/7, unstaffed, keycard-access membership club.**

As someone who researches real estate finance and economic theory—and spends way too many hours practicing 9-ball—this structural shift is fascinating. The death of the traditional urban pool hall and the rise of the automated "PropTech" billiards club is a masterclass in **spatial unit economics, screening contracts, and the theory of club goods.**

---

## 1. The Spatial Curse: The 58-Inch Stroke Tax

To understand why traditional pool halls struggle to survive modern urban retail rents, we have to start with the merciless math of commercial real estate: **Revenue per Square Foot ($\text{Rev/SqFt}$)**.

A regulation 9-foot pool table (like a Diamond Pro-Am or Brunswick Gold Crown) has a playing surface of \\$4.5\text{ ft} \times 9\text{ ft}$, or just **40.5 square feet**. If you could pack pool tables edge-to-edge, billiards would be a goldmine.

Unfortunately, physics gets in the way. Because a standard pool cue is 58 inches long, players require roughly 5 feet of unobstructed clearance on all four sides of the table to take a full backswing. Once you add the table, the stroke perimeter, and minimal walkway clearance, a single 9-foot table consumes roughly **$14\text{ ft} \times 18.5\text{ ft} \approx 260\text{ to }300\text{ square feet}$** of leasable floor space.

Think about the opportunity cost of 300 square feet in a commercial corridor:
* **A Restaurant or Bar:** Fits 16 to 20 dining seats. Turning those seats 2.5 times an evening at a \\$45 check generates **\\$1,800 to \\$2,250 per night**.
* **A Boutique Spin or Pilates Studio:** Fits 12 reformer machines or bikes, generating **\\$35 per person per hour** across multiple classes a day.
* **A 9-Foot Pool Table:** Accommodates **2 people** (maybe 4 in doubles) paying \\$15 to \\$20 per hour *combined*. Even across 6 peak evening hours, table time yields around **\\$100 a night**—roughly **\\$0.33 per square foot per day**.

In real estate finance terms, the air around a pool table is unmonetized dead space. When urban Triple-Net (NNN) retail rents climbed from $15/sq ft to \\$45+/sq ft, pure table-time could no longer pay the landlord.

## 2. Adverse Selection and the Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening Problem

Historically, traditional pool halls solved the spatial math problem through **cross-subsidization**. Table time was treated as a break-even loss leader to anchor customers in the building while the house made its real margin selling high-markup beer, liquor, and fried food.

But relying on a Food & Beverage (F&B) cross-subsidy creates a textbook microeconomic pathology: **adverse selection and misaligned incentives**.

Consider the two archetypes walking into a pool hall:
1. **The Serious Grinder:** Shows up on a Tuesday at 2:00 PM, rents a table for four hours, drinks free tap water, and demands pristine Simonis 860 cloth, clean Aramith balls, and tight pockets. **F&B Margin: $0.**
2. **The Friday Night Crowd:** A group of six casual friends shows up at 10:00 PM, orders three pitchers of beer and nachos, hitting balls around pretty hard, sets sweating pint glasses on the rails or worse, the cloth, and occasionally rips the cloth attempting an illegal jump shot. **F&B Margin: High. Equipment Depreciation: High.**

To survive high ground-floor retail rent and pay for bartenders, kitchen staff, and bouncers, the traditional pool hall is forced to cater to the Friday night drinker. In doing so, the tables deteriorate, driving away the serious players.

### How Membership Clubs Solve This: Screening Contracts
The modern 24/7 membership pool club flips this equilibrium on its head using one of the most famous mechanisms in microeconomics: **Rothschild and Stiglitz's (1976) model of Screening in Competitive Markets**.

In a world of asymmetric information, a pool room owner cannot easily tell at the door whether a new customer is a respectful player or a cloth-ripping rowdy. So instead of policing behavior with security guards, the membership club offers a **separating contract**:
* **No bar, no kitchen, no single-hour walk-ins.**
* **A flat \\$150 to \\$250 monthly recurring membership fee**, plus an upfront onboarding orientation.

This contract acts as a pure **self-selection screening device**. A casual Friday night partier looking to drink beer and bang balls will never pay \\$180/month for a quiet, alcohol-free room with etiquette rules, they self-select out to a sports bar or Topgolf. Conversely, the serious player who practices 15 hours a week views \\$180/month as an absolute bargain.

## 3. Buchanan’s Theory of Clubs and Ostrom’s Commons

Once the membership model screens in the right cohort, how does an *unstaffed* room with \\$10,000 Diamond tables survive 24 hours a day without someone stealing the balls or trashing the equipment?

This brings us to Nobel laureate **James Buchanan’s (1965) *An Economic Theory of Clubs*** and **Elinor Ostrom’s (1990) *Governing the Commons***.

In public economics, goods exist on a spectrum between pure private goods and pure public goods. A membership pool hall is the literal textbook definition of a **Buchanan Club Good**: it is **excludable** (via smartphone smart-lock at the door), and **non-rivalrous up to a congestion threshold** (as long as one of the 8 tables is open, my practice session doesn't hurt yours).

Buchanan showed that the optimal size of a club ($N^*$) is where the marginal benefit of adding one more member (sharing the fixed real estate lease across more wallets) equals the marginal congestion cost (the probability of showing up at 7:00 PM and having to wait for a table). 

Furthermore, transforming customers from hourly spot-market renters into *club members* eliminates **moral hazard**:
* In a traditional hall, an hourly renter faces a classic Tragedy of the Commons—they have zero financial or social stake in whether the cloth is brushed or chalk is left face-down on the rails.
* In a capped 80-member club governed by Ostrom-style community norms (backed by cloud cameras and keycard access logs), members actively brush the tables, clean the balls, and police the space themselves because they share ownership of the club's quality.

## 4. PropTech Arbitrage and "Paying Not to Go to the Gym"

Finally, look at the corporate finance transformation on the income statement. By combining the Club Good model with simple **PropTech automation**—an app-based smart lock, security cameras, and IoT relay switches that automatically flip on the overhead perimeter LED light when a member reserves a table—the business model achieves three massive financial advantages:

1. **Spatial Arbitrage (Class-A Felt in Class-C Space):** Because members navigate to the club intentionally via an app rather than wandering in off the street, the club does not need expensive ground-floor retail visibility. It can lease windowless 2nd-floor Class B/C office space, basement footprints, or light-industrial flex space at **40% to 60% lower rent per square foot**—Wait, no windows? For serious pool players, zero sunlight glare on the table is a feature, not a bug!
2. **CapEx-for-OpEx Labor Substitution:** In a traditional mid-sized room, front-desk and bar payroll consumes 30% to 45% of gross revenue, bleeding cash during dead weekday afternoons. Automation drops on-site variable labor near zero, unlocking **168 hours per week of asset utilization** so night-owl grinders can practice at 2:30 AM at zero marginal cost to the owner.
3. **Cash Flow Smoothing & DellaVigna-Malmendier Breakage:** As **DellaVigna and Malmendier (2006)** famously documented in *Paying Not to Go to the Gym*, flat monthly subscription contracts bundle predictable recurring revenue for the operator with behavioral "breakage" from members who travel or get busy at work for three weeks but keep their membership active to hold their spot on the roster. Fixed lease obligations are covered on the 1st of every month.
